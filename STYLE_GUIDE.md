# Writing Style Guide

This is the voice for all Runner docs, help content, and user-facing copy. It's Kent's voice, pulled from ten posts on [ewakened.com](https://ewakened.com). The goal: it should feel like a real person wrote it, not a content team or an AI.

Docs are the measured version of the blog voice. Same person, indoor voice. Keep the personality, lose the storytelling.

## Core characteristics

**Conversational and direct.** Write like you're talking to someone, not at them. No corporate jargon.

**Practical, not preachy.** Show people how to do things. Don't lecture.

**Short lines for impact, longer lines for flow.** Mix them up. Uniform paragraph lengths are a dead giveaway that nobody human touched the page.

**Trust the reader.** Don't over-explain. If someone is reading the docs, they're already trying to figure it out.

**Opinionated, but honest about it.** The blog says "If I had to guess" and "I would bet" before a hot take. In docs that means: say what Runner does plainly, and don't oversell what it might do.

## Voice patterns from the blog

These are the moves that make the voice recognizable. Use them.

**Open with the concrete thing.** Blog posts open with a moment, not a thesis: "It was December 28th. I woke up at 6am and Cursor had been running all night." Docs pages don't need a story, but they should open with what the reader came for. No preamble, no "In this guide, we'll explore."

**One-line paragraphs as beats.** After a longer paragraph, a short line lands hard.

> You still copy and paste. You still bounce back and forth, moving context from one place to another. You still deal with AI that is more chatty and less do-y.
>
> We were tired of it.

The short lines work because they're surrounded by flow. Use this when a point needs to stick. Not on every page.

**Ask the question, answer it.** "Why the difference? Simply cost." When the reader is about to wonder why, ask it for them and answer in the same breath.

**Repetition for rhythm.** "You still copy and paste. You still bounce back and forth. You still connect and re-connect the same apps." Three parallel sentences beat one long one stuffed with commas.

**Real numbers, real details.** 378 days. December 28th. 6am. "Every weekday at 8am" not "on a regular basis." Specifics make a sentence trustworthy. Vague makes it forgettable.

**Talk to the reader, then point them somewhere.** The blog ends posts with a challenge: "How are you building in 2025?" A docs page ends by telling the reader what to do next.

**Physical analogies for abstract things.** The blog explains delegation with drywall and light switches, search infrastructure with vacuum cleaners, founder judgment with game point in basketball. If a concept is abstract, reach for something physical. One analogy per concept. Don't stack them.

**Bold the line they must not skim past.** "Always review complex code with another agent." When there's a rule that matters, bold it and give it its own line.

**Parenthetical asides, sparingly.** "(we are in Canada people)". In docs, one per page at most. It's seasoning, not sauce.

The blog signs off with "There's more work tomorrow. Enjoy tonight." That one stays on the blog. Don't put it in docs pages.

## What to do

- Write like you talk
- Use contractions ("you're", "it's", "don't")
- Keep sentences varied in length
- Use second person ("you") by default
- Bold for UI elements: Click **Settings**
- Code formatting for file names, commands, paths
- Be specific. "Every weekday at 8am" not "on a regular basis"
- When something is simple, say so and move on

## What to avoid

- Em dashes. Use periods, commas, or line breaks instead. "Runner checks your apps. Then gives you a brief." not "Runner checks your apps — then gives you a brief."
- Corporate jargon ("leverage", "synergy", "circle back", "from day one", "set yourself up for success")
- Fake enthusiasm or cheerleading ("Runner nails it!", "game changer")
- "Period." as a standalone emphasis word
- Passive voice ("it was decided")
- Long dense paragraphs without breathing room
- Hiding behind complexity
- Overly polished transitions ("Here's what that looks like in practice:")
- Tidy consultant-speak lists ("Alignment. Impact. Communication.") that could have come from any blog on the internet
- Adding "knowledge workers" when you can just describe the people

## Section headers

Direct and conversational. Not marketing taglines. The blog uses headers like "Unless it's game point" and "What Apple needs to do... tomorrow".

- "Runner doesn't suggest. It finishes." (good)
- "Stop asking. Start finishing." (too tagline-y)
- "What are you looking for?" (good)
- "Unlock Your Productivity Potential" (no)

## Card and link descriptions

Keep these tight. One line. Don't repeat the title.

- "Quick wins and features you might not have found yet." (good)
- "Quick wins and hidden features to speed up your day and maximize your productivity." (too much)

## Copy-paste / template messages

When writing example messages users can copy-paste (referral messages, email templates, etc.), write them the way a real person texts or emails. No marketing polish.

- "Hey, I've been using this app called Runner..." (good)
- "I wanted to reach out and share an exciting productivity tool..." (no)

## Security and trust sections

Be direct and confident without being corporate. Say what you do and don't do. Don't add filler.

- "We don't train models on your data." (good)
- "Your data is never used to improve or train artificial intelligence models, ensuring complete privacy." (too much)

## Tone scale

This is a docs/help site, not a blog. Keep the personality present but measured.

| Too dry | Just right | Too much |
|---------|-----------|----------|
| "Configure the automation parameters." | "Tell Runner what you want and when." | "Runner is SO excited to automate your life!" |
| "Refer to the installation documentation." | "Download Runner and open it." | "Let's gooo! Time to install!" |
