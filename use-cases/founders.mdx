A morning brief is useful. A brief that tells you which proposal went quiet, which customer promise is slipping, and which number needs a decision is much more useful.

That's the move with Runner. Start with personal work you already understand. Then aim the same system at the parts of the business that make or lose money.

<Note>
  Writes follow your [tool permissions](/connections/tool-permissions). **Ask** stages an **Action Card** for review. **Allow** acts without asking. **Off** blocks the tool unless a user-created Runner has its own override.
</Note>

## Put the important work on a schedule

| Runner | What it checks | Useful result |
|--------|----------------|---------------|
| **Executive watchlist** | Gmail, Calendar, CRM, project tracker, and your metrics sheet | A ranked list of revenue, customer, cost, and delivery decisions |
| **Revenue follow-through** | Open proposals, deals without a next step, and unanswered threads | The three opportunities most likely to need action today |
| **Customer risk** | Recent customer email, open issues, commitments, and renewal dates | A short list of accounts that need an owner or response |
| **Weekly operating review** | Team metrics, open projects, hiring, cash, and blockers | One report that names what changed and where you need to decide |
| **Board and investor update** | Approved metrics, milestones, hiring notes, and prior update format | A first draft based on approved sources, with missing inputs called out |

The point isn't a prettier summary. It's catching the thing you would have found next Thursday and seeing it today.

## Build this first: executive watchlist

```text
Create a Runner named Executive Watchlist.

Every weekday at 7:30am in my timezone, review my connected Gmail, Google
Calendar, [CRM], [project tracker], and the current operating metrics in
[Google Sheet].

Only include items that need my attention in the next 7 days:
1. Revenue: new leads, proposals, or open deals with no clear next step.
2. Customers: commitments, risks, or questions waiting on us.
3. Cost: unusual changes or deadlines shown in the sources.
4. Delivery: blocked work, missed dates, or decisions waiting on me.
5. Today: meetings that need preparation.

For every item, cite the source, explain why it matters, name the owner if one
is shown, and give me one recommended next step. Don't invent a cause, owner,
deadline, or dollar value. If the source is unclear, say so.

Don't send messages or update records. Draft anything useful for my review.
Return no more than 10 items. Rank them by money at risk and how soon I need to act.

Before you create it, show me the final name, prompt, schedule, and timezone.
Wait for my confirmation.
```

**A user-created Runner becomes active as soon as it's created.** Review its schedule and permissions, then run it once. If the list is full of noise, don't ask Runner to "be smarter." Tighten the sources, thresholds, and exclusions in the saved prompt.

## Give it the business context

Create a Project for the operating context this Runner should use:

- Current priorities and the metrics you actually care about.
- Your definition of an at-risk customer or stalled deal.
- Who owns which part of the business.
- The format for operating, board, and investor updates.
- Decisions Runner should never make for you.

The Project holds standing context. Your CRM, inbox, project tracker, and metrics sheet hold live status. Each run starts in a separate session, so don't rely on yesterday's output being there tomorrow.

Keep write tools on **Ask** while the Runner is learning your business. A suggested email can wait in an Action Card. The final decision is still yours.

## Build the next Runner

### Proposal and pipeline follow-through

Check open revenue every weekday morning. Find proposals with no reply and deals with no next step. Stage a specific follow-up using the real thread and account context.

### Weekly operating review

Run Friday afternoon. Compare the approved metrics with the prior period, pull blockers from the project tracker, and draft the questions that need an answer. Don't claim a cause unless a source states it.

### Customer risk review

Run twice a week. Look for unresolved customer questions, missed commitments, open high-priority issues, and renewals approaching. Rank the list, then name an owner.

### Board or investor update assembly

Run on the date you normally start each monthly or quarterly update. Pull approved metrics and milestones into the existing format. Flag every missing number instead of guessing.

## Build toward the business

Your Personal Assistant can give you a better morning. These Runners can keep revenue, customers, and decisions from slipping between meetings.

That's where the bigger value lives.
